Post-Identity Design: Brands, Politics, and Technological Instability

Federico Pérez Villoro and Christopher Hamamoto. March 23, 2017.

The article “Post-Identity Design: Brands, Politics, and Technological Instability” by Federico Pérez Villoro and Christopher Hamamoto discusses the changing role of design in a world shaped by technology, politics, and globalization.

The authors begin by discussing how Google redesigned its logo so it could load faster across different devices and internet connections. Google had created thousands of possible versions of the logo so it could work in different situations, showing that modern identity design is not just about appearance, but it is also about technology and accessibility. This shows how modern branding should work across many different platforms and situations as technology adapts.

“The relative simplicity of the new logo, then, was about more than just the formal, aesthetic qualities of the mark. A major part of it was about the desire for pixel-perfect efficiency and cross-platform accessibility—the need to accommodate the thousands of possible scenarios triggered by computers accessing the logo.”

However, the author argues that design should be looked at politically, not just aesthetically. For example, companies like Google benefit from expanding Internet access because more users means more business and growth. Technology companies also have more influence over information and society. For example, on a political scale, nation branding uses advertising and design to make countries appear attractive and competitive, like how companies build brands. Politicians increasingly use branding and marketing techniques to build their public image and connect with voters, treating political campaigns much like commercial brands.

“Furthermore, we need to analyze these actions beyond the corporate sphere and within a political perspective, since developments in technology are blurring distinctions between private and public entities and driving complex shifts in notions of agency and power.”

Technology and information are also becoming increasingly connected to these forms of power through influence. The Internet and cloud computing allow governments and companies to influence people and information beyond their physical territory. This is seen very commonly today, as the filtering of news circulation and the wording used to frame stories can influence how people understand certain events.

“The current capitalist hegemony is leading toward increased precarity and geopolitical chaos. If design can contribute to the construction of a different reality, it must first question its own mechanisms that sustain the neoliberal agenda, legitimize traditional brands’ power, and enable the flow of misinformation.”

The authors argue that design should question its global impact and its own role in supporting existing systems of power. Current capitalism can contribute to inequality and insecurity, so designers should consider how branding and technology may reinforce these issues or contribute to the spread of misinformation. Instead, design can be used to create systems that give people more access to information and greater control. For example, designers can create interfaces that allow people to exchange ideas and different perspectives directly. As designers, we should think carefully about who controls technology, how it shapes people’s behavior, and how to adapt our designs to current technological and societal states.
